# Does E85 Run Cooler? Engine Temperature Myths Debunked

When diving into the world of automotive performance and alternative fuels, one of the most frequently discussed topics is E85. Known for its high octane rating and potential to unlock significant horsepower gains, E85 has become the fuel of choice for many tuners and enthusiasts. However, alongside the horsepower discussions, a persistent question often arises: **Does E85 run cooler?**

The short answer is yes, but the long answer is far more complex and interesting. The relationship between E85 and engine temperature is surrounded by scientific facts, misinterpretations, and outright myths. If you are considering making the switch to ethanol-based fuels, understanding how it affects your engine's thermal dynamics is crucial. 

In this comprehensive guide, we will break down the science behind ethanol combustion, explore how E85 affects different temperature metrics within your engine, and debunk the most common myths surrounding E85 and engine cooling.

---

## What is E85 Fuel?

Before we delve into the thermal properties of the fuel, it is essential to understand what E85 actually is. E85 is an alternative fuel blend consisting of up to 85% ethanol and 15% gasoline (or other hydrocarbon blendstocks). Ethanol is a renewable biofuel typically made from plant materials such as corn, sugarcane, or grasses.

In the context of automotive performance, E85 is highly prized for two main characteristics:
1. **High Octane Rating:** E85 typically boasts an octane rating of 100 to 105. This high resistance to detonation (engine knock) allows tuners to run more aggressive ignition timing and higher boost pressures, leading to significant power increases.
2. **Cooling Properties:** As we will explore in detail, ethanol has unique chemical properties that allow it to absorb heat more effectively than standard gasoline.

It is worth noting that the actual ethanol content in pump E85 can vary depending on the season and region, sometimes dipping as low as 51% (often referred to as E51) during winter months to aid in cold starting. Regardless of the exact blend, the presence of a high percentage of ethanol fundamentally changes how the engine operates thermally.

## The Science Behind Ethanol and Cooling

To understand whether E85 makes your engine run cooler, we must look at the thermodynamics of fuel vaporization and combustion. There are two primary scientific principles at play here: the Latent Heat of Vaporization and combustion temperatures.

### 1. Latent Heat of Vaporization

The most significant factor contributing to E85's cooling effect is its **latent heat of vaporization**. 

Latent heat of vaporization refers to the amount of energy (in the form of heat) required to change a substance from a liquid state into a gas. For a fuel to combust in an engine, it must first vaporize and mix with air. During this phase change from liquid to vapor, the fuel absorbs heat from its surroundings—in this case, the intake manifold, the intake valves, and the combustion chamber itself.

Here is the critical difference:
*   **Gasoline** has a relatively low latent heat of vaporization (roughly 350 kJ/kg).
*   **Ethanol** has a much higher latent heat of vaporization (roughly 840 kJ/kg).

Because ethanol requires more than twice as much heat energy to vaporize compared to gasoline, it absorbs significantly more heat from the intake air charge as it is injected. Furthermore, because E85 has a lower energy density than gasoline, you must inject about 30% to 40% more fuel by volume to achieve the correct air-to-fuel ratio. 

Combining the higher latent heat with the increased volume of fuel injected means that E85 pulls a massive amount of thermal energy out of the intake charge. This results in a drastically cooler, denser air-fuel mixture entering the cylinders.

### 2. Combustion Temperatures

The second aspect is the actual temperature of the combustion event. Ethanol burns cooler than standard pump gasoline. The flame temperature of ethanol is lower, meaning the peak temperatures reached inside the cylinder during the power stroke are reduced. 

This cooler combustion process puts less thermal stress on internal engine components such as the pistons, exhaust valves, and the cylinder head itself.

## Does E85 Actually Make Your Engine Run Cooler?

So, returning to the main question: Does E85 run cooler? We need to look at specific temperature metrics within the engine to get a complete picture.

### Intake Charge Cooling (Yes)

As explained by the latent heat of vaporization, E85 absolutely excels at intake charge cooling. If you measure the Intake Air Temperature (IAT) in a forced-induction engine (turbocharged or supercharged), injecting E85 can significantly lower the temperature of the air entering the cylinders. 

This cooler intake charge is denser, packing more oxygen molecules into the same volume, which is a primary reason why E85 makes more power. The cooling effect acts almost like a chemical intercooler, dropping temperatures right before combustion.

### Exhaust Gas Temperatures / EGTs (Yes)

Because ethanol has a lower combustion temperature, the resulting exhaust gases are also cooler. Exhaust Gas Temperatures (EGTs) are a critical metric for engine health, particularly in turbocharged applications where extreme EGTs can damage the turbocharger turbine, exhaust valves, and manifolds.

Switching from pump gas to E85 typically results in noticeably lower EGTs, sometimes by hundreds of degrees Fahrenheit, depending on the tune. This lower thermal load is incredibly beneficial for component longevity and reliability under heavy track or racing use.

### Engine Coolant and Oil Temperatures (It Depends)

This is where the nuances begin. While the intake charge and combustion temperatures are cooler, does this mean your dashboard temperature gauge will read lower? 

Generally, E85 *can* lower your overall engine coolant and oil temperatures, but the effect is often less dramatic than the drop in EGTs. The cooling system (radiator, thermostat, water pump) is designed to regulate the engine at a specific operating temperature. If your thermostat is set to open at 190°F, the cooling system will strive to maintain that temperature regardless of the fuel used.

However, during sustained high-load situations (like lapping a track), where the cooling system might struggle to keep up with the heat generated by gasoline, E85's cooler combustion can provide enough thermal relief to prevent the coolant and oil from overheating. In daily driving scenarios, your coolant temp gauge will likely read exactly the same on E85 as it does on standard gas.

## Common Myths Surrounding E85 and Engine Temperature

With a foundational understanding of the science, let us debunk some of the most pervasive myths about E85 and engine cooling.

### Myth 1: E85 Fixes Overheating Issues

**The Myth:** If your car is overheating on the street or track due to a failing water pump, a clogged radiator, or poor airflow, switching to E85 will solve the problem.

**The Reality:** E85 is a fuel, not a mechanical fix. While it generates less heat during combustion, it will not compensate for a fundamentally flawed or failing cooling system. If your radiator is too small for your power level, or your thermostat is stuck closed, E85 might delay the overheating slightly, but it will not prevent it. You must address the root mechanical cause of the overheating. E85 provides a thermal buffer, not a mechanical repair.

### Myth 2: You Need to Change Your Thermostat for E85

**The Myth:** Because E85 runs cooler, you need to install a higher-temperature thermostat so the engine can reach its proper operating temperature, or conversely, you need a colder thermostat to take full advantage of the cooling.

**The Reality:** In most applications, you should stick with the OEM recommended thermostat temperature. Modern fuel injection systems rely on coolant temperature data to adjust fueling. If the engine runs too cold, the ECU may stay in the "warm-up" enrichment phase, leading to poor fuel economy and accelerated cylinder wear. The engine's cooling system will still regulate temperatures effectively with the stock thermostat on E85.

### Myth 3: E85 Causes Engines to Run "Too Cold" in Winter

**The Myth:** The cooling properties of E85 are so strong that the engine will never warm up in cold weather, leading to damage.

**The Reality:** The primary cold-weather issue with E85 is not running temperature, but rather **cold starting**. Ethanol has a low vapor pressure at cold temperatures, meaning it doesn't vaporize easily when the engine is freezing cold. This makes the initial start difficult. 

However, once the engine fires up and runs for a few moments, combustion generates heat, and the engine will warm up to its normal operating temperature regulated by the thermostat. The myth confuses cold start difficulty with operating temperature. To combat cold start issues, winter blends of E85 often reduce the ethanol content to 51%-70% to increase gasoline content and improve vaporization.

### Myth 4: E85 Burns Hotter Because It Makes More Power

**The Myth:** More horsepower equals more heat. Since E85 makes significantly more power than pump gas, the engine must be running much hotter.

**The Reality:** This is a misunderstanding of how the power is generated. The power increase from E85 comes from the ability to advance ignition timing (due to higher octane) and run higher boost pressures, combined with the dense, cold intake charge. While creating more horsepower *does* generally produce more total heat energy, the specific properties of ethanol (high latent heat of vaporization and lower flame temperature) offset this. The thermal efficiency of the combustion event is improved, meaning less heat is wasted into the engine block and exhaust, and more is used to push the piston down.

## The True Benefits of E85's Cooling Effect

Now that we have separated fact from fiction, let's explore why E85's cooling capabilities are so highly valued by performance enthusiasts and engine builders.

### 1. Knock Prevention and Higher Octane

Engine knock (detonation) is the uncontrolled explosion of the air-fuel mixture, which can quickly destroy an engine. Heat is a primary catalyst for knock. Hot spots in the combustion chamber or a high intake air temperature can cause the fuel to ignite prematurely.

E85's massive cooling effect on the intake charge, combined with its inherently high octane rating, creates an incredibly knock-resistant environment. The cooler the air-fuel mixture, the less likely it is to detonate under high pressure.

### 2. Increased Ignition Timing and Power

Because E85 suppresses knock so effectively, engine tuners can significantly advance the ignition timing. Advancing the timing means the spark plug fires earlier in the compression stroke, allowing the expanding combustion gases more time to push down on the piston, extracting more mechanical work (horsepower) from the fuel. 

On pump gas, advancing the timing too far leads to knock. E85 provides the thermal safety margin needed to extract maximum power.

### 3. Safer Forced Induction

Turbochargers and superchargers generate immense amounts of heat by compressing air. This hot, compressed air must usually be cooled by an intercooler before entering the engine. When running high boost on pump gas, the temperatures can still reach dangerous levels.

The "chemical intercooling" effect of E85 is a game-changer for forced induction. It cools the intake charge so aggressively that engines can run much higher boost levels safely. In some drag racing applications running methanol (a close relative to ethanol with even stronger cooling properties), engines don't even use physical intercoolers because the fuel cools the charge enough on its own.

### 4. Engine Longevity Under Stress

High Exhaust Gas Temperatures (EGTs) are the enemy of exhaust valves, turbo manifolds, and turbine housings. Sustained high EGTs can cause metals to warp, crack, or melt. Because E85 burns cooler, it dramatically lowers EGTs. 

For a track car that spends 20 minutes at wide-open throttle, the lower EGTs provided by E85 can mean the difference between a reliable engine and a melted turbocharger. It reduces the thermal fatigue on critical components.

## Considerations When Switching to E85

While the cooling and performance benefits are undeniable, E85 is not a simple "pour and play" solution for every vehicle. Taking advantage of these thermal properties requires proper preparation.

### Fuel System Upgrades

Because E85 has less energy per gallon than gasoline, you must inject significantly more fuel to achieve the correct mixture. This requires:
*   **Larger Fuel Injectors:** To flow the increased volume of fuel.
*   **Upgraded Fuel Pump(s):** To maintain fuel pressure at higher flow rates.
*   **Ethanol-Compatible Components:** Ethanol is corrosive to certain older rubbers and plastics. Modern vehicles (typically post-2000) usually have compatible lines, but it is crucial to verify.

### Tuning Requirements

To harness the cooling effects and higher octane, the engine's Engine Control Unit (ECU) must be calibrated (tuned) for E85. The tune adjusts the fueling tables to supply the extra volume required and advances the ignition timing to make power safely. 

Many modern setups utilize a **Flex-Fuel sensor**, which measures the exact percentage of ethanol in the fuel line in real-time. The ECU then interpolates between a gasoline tune and an E85 tune, allowing the driver to mix fuels seamlessly without changing maps manually.

### Fuel Consumption

The trade-off for cooler temperatures and more power is reduced fuel economy. Because you need to burn roughly 30% more E85 than gasoline to travel the same distance, your miles per gallon (MPG) will decrease noticeably. However, for performance enthusiasts, the drastic increase in horsepower and thermal safety more than offsets the cost at the pump.

## Conclusion: The Thermal Advantage of E85

So, does E85 run cooler? The definitive answer is **yes**. 

Through the physics of its high latent heat of vaporization, E85 drastically cools the incoming air charge, acting as a powerful chemical intercooler. Its lower combustion temperatures translate directly to lower Exhaust Gas Temperatures, reducing thermal stress on internal engine parts and turbochargers. 

While it will not fix a mechanically broken radiator or magically drop your dashboard coolant gauge to zero, E85 provides a massive thermal safety net. It prevents engine knock, allows for aggressive tuning, and creates a more thermally stable environment under extreme performance conditions. 

By debunking the myths and understanding the science, it becomes clear why E85 is often referred to as "liquid horsepower." The cooling effect is not just a byproduct; it is the very mechanism that allows engines to safely produce reliable, massive power gains. If your fuel system is equipped to handle it and your ECU is properly tuned, the thermal advantages of E85 are impossible to ignore.
